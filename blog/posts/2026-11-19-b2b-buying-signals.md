Most writing about B2B buying signals is about software: who visited your pricing page, who downloaded a white paper, which accounts a data vendor says are "surging" on a topic. If you sell emissions testing, process piping or a compliance platform for food plants, those signals barely exist. Your buyers do not research in public. They ask a peer, call the supplier they already know, and send out a request for quotes when the decision is mostly made.

They do leave a trail, though. It is in permits, job postings, filings, inspection records and announcements, and it usually appears months before anyone asks for a quote. This post is a working list of those signals: what each one means, where to find it, how to score it and what to do when one shows up.

## What counts as a buying signal

A buying signal is a public, dated event at a specific company that tends to come before a purchase of what you sell. Each word in that sentence does work:

- **Public.** You can see it without the company telling you. That rules out "they seemed interested on the call".
- **Dated.** It happened on a known day, so you can tell whether it is fresh.
- **Specific.** It belongs to one company, ideally one site. "Food manufacturing is growing in Ohio" is a trend, not a signal.
- **Comes before a purchase.** This is the test most signals fail. A company posting on LinkedIn about its anniversary is public, dated and specific, and tells you nothing.

The reason signals matter so much in a small market is timing. The LinkedIn B2B Institute and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute popularized the "95-5 rule": at any given time, roughly 95% of business buyers are not in the market for a given category. Signals are how you work out which accounts are in the other 5% this quarter, without waiting for them to call.

## The B2B buying signals worth tracking, and where to find them

There are seven families that cover most technical and industrial sales. You will not need all of them.

### 1. Hiring

A job posting is a company describing its plans in detail, in public, for free. Read the title and the body.

- A first-ever posting for a quality manager or EHS manager often means a customer, an auditor or a regulator has asked for something the company cannot yet deliver.
- A cluster of operator or technician postings at one site means added capacity or a new shift.
- The body text names equipment, software, standards and certifications the company runs or wants.

**Where:** the company's own careers page, the large job boards, and state workforce job banks.

### 2. Permits and regulatory filings

Before a company can build, expand or change a process, it usually has to ask someone for permission, and the request is a public record.

- **Building permits** show new construction, additions and fit-outs, with a declared project value and often the contractor's name. Most cities and counties publish them through an online portal.
- **Environmental permits** (air, wastewater, stormwater) show new or changed processes. The EPA's ECHO database lists regulated facilities, their permits and compliance history; state environmental agencies publish applications and public notices.

Permits are among the earliest signals available. An air permit application for a new coating line can be filed a year before the line runs.

### 3. Expansion and investment

- **State and local incentive awards.** Economic development agencies announce the company, the site, the investment and the promised job count.
- **Funding.** Private companies raising money in the US generally file a Form D with the SEC, searchable in EDGAR. Announced rounds and acquisitions show up in trade press.
- **Equipment financing.** UCC filings, held by each state's secretary of state, record liens on financed equipment. They show a company has just bought machinery, and sometimes what kind.

### 4. Leadership changes

A new plant manager, VP of operations, engineering director or quality head typically reviews suppliers and open problems in their first few months. They have a reason to change things and no loyalty to the current vendor.

**Where:** LinkedIn job changes, company press releases, trade publication "people" columns, and the careers page (a senior role that disappears has been filled).

### 5. Compliance events

A problem with a deadline is the strongest reason to buy.

- **Inspections and violations.** OSHA publishes inspection records by establishment. EPA ECHO shows violations and enforcement actions. The FDA publishes warning letters and inspection classifications.
- **Certification activity.** A company announcing it is pursuing ISO 9001, AS9100, ISO 17025 or a customer-required standard has a list of gaps to close. Accreditation bodies publish who holds what, so new entries and scope changes are visible.

Handle these with care in outreach. Nobody enjoys a stranger opening with their violation. Lead with the requirement and the fix, not the finding.

### 6. Procurement activity

- **RFPs and bid notices.** SAM.gov for federal work, state and municipal procurement portals, and private bid boards in construction.
- **Award records.** USAspending.gov shows who won federal contracts and when those contracts end. An expiring contract is a dated signal for the rebid.

An RFP is a late signal. By the time it is published, someone has usually helped write the specification. Use procurement records mainly to see what is coming up for renewal.

### 7. Vendor and technology changes

- A supplier being acquired, discontinuing a product line or closing a regional office leaves its customers looking.
- A company announcing a new ERP, a new production line or a move to a different material or process creates work for everyone downstream of that change.

**Where:** trade press, supplier announcements, distributor notices and the job postings from family 1, which often name the system being replaced.

## How to choose your three to five

Do not start from the list above. Start from your own wins.

1. Take your last ten to fifteen customers.
2. For each, write down what happened at that company in the six to twelve months before they bought. Ask the account owner, check the news, look at their permit and hiring history.
3. Count which events repeat.
4. Keep the three to five that appear most often and that you can check from a public source every week.

A firm that installs dust collection systems will probably land on building permits, air permit applications, OSHA combustible dust citations and maintenance manager hires. A calibration lab might land on new ISO 17025 or AS9100 pursuits, quality manager hires, FDA inspection outcomes and new production lines. A vertical software company selling to freight brokers might track new operating authorities, funding and operations leadership hires. The lists are different because the sales are different.

## Score signals so the list stays short

A raw feed of signals is noise. A simple score makes it usable.

**Strength.** Rate each signal type from 1 to 3 by how often it preceded a past win. A permit for the exact process you serve is a 3. A general hiring increase is a 1.

**Freshness.** Signals expire. A reasonable default: full value for 30 days, half value up to 90 days, nothing after 180. Adjust to your sales cycle; a permit for a two-year construction project stays useful longer than a job posting.

**Stacking.** Two different signals at one account are worth more than their sum. A building permit plus a posting for a process engineer plus a new operations leader is a company in the middle of a project.

Then sort accounts into three groups:

- **Quiet:** no current signals. Leave them alone apart from occasional useful contact.
- **Warming:** one signal. Watch closely and research the people.
- **In market:** two or more signals, or one strong one. Reach out this week.

In a market of 800 companies, the in-market group in any given week is typically a few dozen accounts. That is a list a small team can actually work.

## What to do in the week a signal appears

Speed matters because the same permit is visible to your competitors, and because the early conversations shape the specification.

1. **Confirm it.** Open the source record. Check the site, the date and the scope. Aggregated data is often wrong about which location is involved.
2. **Work out what it means for your product.** A 40,000 square foot addition means more compressed air, more dust, more inspection work, more of whatever you supply. Put a rough number on it.
3. **Find the buying group.** The person running the project, the technical evaluator, the budget owner and purchasing. Permit applications and press releases often name the first one.
4. **Write to each person with the reason attached.** "I saw the permit application for the addition on Route 9. Projects of that size usually need X decided before the slab is poured. Here is what we would check first." Each person gets the angle that matches their job.
5. **Follow up across channels** over two to three weeks: email, phone, LinkedIn, and a letter for senior people.
6. **Record what happened**, so that in six months you know which signals produced conversations and can adjust the scores.

## Signal checklist

- We have three to five signal types, chosen from what happened before our past wins.
- Each one has a named public source and a named person who checks it.
- The check happens every week, against our full account list. (No list yet? Start with [how to find customers in a niche B2B market](/blog/find-customers-niche-b2b-market/).)
- Each signal type has a strength rating and an expiry.
- Accounts are sorted into quiet, warming and in market, and the in-market list is short enough to work.
- Every outreach message states the specific event that prompted it.
- We review each quarter which signals led to conversations, and drop the ones that did not.
