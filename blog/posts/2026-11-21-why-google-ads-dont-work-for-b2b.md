If you are asking why Google Ads don't work for B2B, your story probably goes like this. A company that sells industrial filtration, structural inspection or a compliance platform for food plants spends a few months on search ads. The form fills come in: students, job seekers, vendors pitching their own services, a homeowner who wanted a different kind of filter. One or two real inquiries arrive, both from companies that already knew the name. The owner concludes that the agency was bad or the keywords were wrong.

Sometimes that is true. More often the account was set up competently and the problem is structural. This post explains why search ads fail for companies that sell into small, specialised markets, gives you a five-question test to check your own situation, and lays out what to do with the money instead.

One caveat first. Google Ads work well for plenty of B2B companies: those with tens of thousands of possible customers, short sales cycles and buyers who search by category. This post is about the other kind of business: a few hundred to a few thousand possible customers, deals of $10k and up, and a purchase that several people have to agree on.

## Why Google Ads don't work for B2B in a small market

There are five reasons, and they compound.

### 1. Most of your market is not searching this quarter

Search ads only reach people at the moment they type a query. The LinkedIn B2B Institute and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute popularised the "95-5 rule": at any given time, roughly 95% of business buyers are not in the market for a given category. If your whole market is 800 companies, that leaves about 40 in a buying window. Only some of those 40 will open a search engine, and only some of those will type a phrase you bid on.

A consumer brand can live on a thin slice of a huge market. A thin slice of 800 is a handful of searches a month.

### 2. The bidding system has nothing to learn from

Automated bidding works by watching which clicks turn into conversions and finding more people like them. Google's own guidance has long recommended on the order of 30 conversions a month before relying on target-CPA bidding. A niche industrial account typically produces a few real conversions a month, and often fewer.

With so little data the system either stalls or optimises toward whatever converts most easily. That is usually the wrong people: job seekers filling in the contact form, students downloading a spec sheet, suppliers using your quote form to pitch you. The platform reports a falling cost per lead while the sales team reports that none of them are buyers.

### 3. Your keywords belong to several other audiences

Technical terms are shared. "Pressure vessel inspection" is typed by plant engineers, and also by people studying for a certification, people looking for inspector jobs, and people writing a report. "Dust collector" is typed by a grain elevator operator and by a hobbyist woodworker. Match types and negative keyword lists help, but in a low-volume account the irrelevant searches typically outnumber the relevant ones no matter how carefully the list is kept.

You pay for every click, and in a small market most of the people clicking were never able to buy.

### 4. The person who clicks is rarely the group that decides

Gartner's research on B2B buying describes a typical buying group for a complex purchase as six to ten people. In a plant that might be the plant manager, the maintenance lead, an engineer, the EHS manager, purchasing and a finance approver.

An ad reaches one of them, often the most junior, who was asked to "find some options". A form fill gives you one name and no view of the other people who will shape the decision. Then the lead is judged on whether that one person replies to a follow-up email.

### 5. By the time they search, the shortlist is mostly set

Buyers in technical markets do most of their thinking away from vendors. Gartner has reported that buying groups spend only about 17% of their purchase time meeting with potential suppliers, and that share is split across every supplier under consideration. In practice the plant manager asks a peer, calls the supplier already on site, and looks at whoever exhibited at the last trade show.

When a search does happen, it is often to find a phone number for a company already on the list, or to collect a third quote that purchasing requires. Winning that click gets you into a comparison you were added to late.

## The arithmetic, with your own numbers

Run this on paper before deciding anything. The figures below are an illustration, not data from a real account. Replace each one with yours.

- **Market size:** 1,200 companies that could buy.
- **In a buying window this year:** assume one in ten, so 120.
- **Share that will run a non-brand search you could bid on:** assume one in four, so 30 companies a year, or two to three a month.
- **Share of those that click your ad rather than a competitor's or an organic result:** assume one in five. That is about six companies a year.
- **Share of those that fill in a form:** assume one in five again. That is one or two real leads a year from paid search.

Now the other side. A small technical campaign will still spend its daily budget, because the platform will find *someone* to show the ad to. Twelve months of spend divided by one or two real leads is your true cost per lead.

You can argue with every assumption, and you should. The point is that when you start from a small number of companies, each multiplication takes you closer to zero, and no setting inside the ad account changes the starting number.

## A five-question test for your own account

Answer these honestly. Four or five "yes" answers mean search ads are unlikely to become a main source of pipeline for you.

1. Could you list every company that could buy from you in one spreadsheet of under 5,000 rows?
2. Is a typical deal worth $10k or more, with more than three people involved in the decision?
3. Do your last ten customers trace back to referrals, existing relationships, trade shows or direct contact, rather than an inbound search?
4. When you read your search terms report, are most of the queries from people who could never buy?
5. Does your account produce fewer than 15 genuine sales conversations a month from paid search?

If you answered "no" to most of them, your market is probably large enough for ads to work, and the fix is inside the account.

## What ads are still good for

A structural limit does not make ads useless. Two uses hold up in a small market:

- **Brand search.** If a competitor bids on your company name, a small campaign on your own name is cheap protection. Buyers who already know you should find you.
- **A narrow, high-intent term.** If there is one phrase that only a real buyer would type (often a standard number plus the word "testing" or "supplier" plus a region), a tightly limited campaign on that phrase can pay for itself. Cap the budget and judge it by sales conversations, not form fills.

Keep both small. Neither will find the 95% who are not searching.

## What to do with the budget instead

When the market is small enough to list, stop waiting for buyers to find you and go to them in order of likelihood.

1. **Build the full account list.** Every company that fits, from industry directories, association member lists, licence and permit registers and trade show exhibitor lists. The method is in [how to find customers in a niche B2B market](/blog/find-customers-niche-b2b-market/).
2. **Track the events that come before a purchase.** A permit application, a first-ever EHS manager job posting, a new plant manager, a funding announcement, an RFP. These tell you which accounts are entering the 5% before they search for anything. See [B2B buying signals: what to track in a niche market](/blog/b2b-buying-signals/).
3. **Map the buying group.** For each flagged account, name the six to ten people who will weigh in, with their roles and a way to reach each one.
4. **Reach them directly, with a reason.** A short message that names the event you saw and what it usually means for someone in their role. One specific email or call does more than a year of impressions.
5. **Review it weekly.** Which accounts showed a signal, who was contacted, what came back. A small market rewards attention more than volume.

This costs time rather than clicks, and the list and the knowledge of the market stay with you once the work is done.
