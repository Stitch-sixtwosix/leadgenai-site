Most guides on how to find decision makers at a company assume your buyers live in a contact database: filter by title, export the list, start sending. That works when you sell software to marketing teams. It works badly when your buyer is a maintenance manager at a food plant, the owner of a 40-person mechanical contractor, or the quality director at a medical device maker. Half of those people have a thin LinkedIn profile or none, their titles vary from one company to the next, and the database record is often two jobs out of date.

This post is a method for niche B2B markets: decide which roles you need, find the names in places that actually carry them, and check them before anyone sends a message.

## Why "the decision maker" is the wrong target

There is rarely one decision maker. Gartner's research on B2B buying has for years put the typical buying group for a complex purchase at six to ten people. In a small or mid-sized company the number is lower, but the roles still exist, even when one person holds two of them.

For most technical sales, the roles are:

- **The person with the problem.** They feel the pain day to day: a plant engineer, a lab manager, a project manager, an operations lead.
- **The technical evaluator.** They write or check the specification: engineering, quality, EHS, IT.
- **The economic owner.** They approve the spend: owner, general manager, VP of operations, a division president.
- **Purchasing.** They run vendor approval, terms and paperwork.
- **The blocker.** Anyone who can say no late in the process: safety, corporate standards, legal, an incumbent vendor's internal champion.

If you only reach the economic owner, the deal stalls when they forward your email to someone who has never heard of you. If you only reach the person with the problem, you get enthusiasm with no budget. The goal is a named person for each role at every account you are working.

## Step 1: Write the role map before you search

Titles change by industry and by company size, so write down what each role is usually called in your market before you look for anyone. A few examples:

- **Industrial suppliers selling to plants:** maintenance manager, reliability engineer, plant manager, purchasing or category manager, EHS manager. (More detail in [lead generation for industrial suppliers](/blog/lead-generation-industrial-suppliers/).)
- **Specialty contractors:** facilities director or property manager at the owner, project manager and estimator at the general contractor, the architect or engineer of record who writes the spec.
- **Engineering firms:** capital projects manager, director of engineering, public works director for municipal clients, the procurement officer who runs the RFP. (More in [how to generate leads for an engineering firm](/blog/lead-generation-engineering-firm/).)
- **Testing, inspection and certification firms:** quality manager, regulatory affairs manager, EHS manager, the corporate compliance lead at multi-site companies.
- **Vertical software:** the owner or general manager at small firms, the operations manager who runs the current system, the office manager or controller who handles billing.

For companies under about 50 people, expect two or three names to cover every role: the owner, an operations or project lead, and whoever runs the office. For multi-site companies, add a corporate layer: someone at headquarters may set standards or approve vendors for every site.

## How to find decision makers at a company: where the names are

In a niche market the names are usually public. They are just not in one place. These are the sources that carry them, roughly in the order worth checking.

### 1. The company's own website and documents

Look past the "About" page. Press releases name the people behind expansions and new contracts. News pages and newsletters name project leads. Sustainability reports, quality policies and supplier manuals often name the EHS or quality lead and sometimes purchasing. Product spec sheets and case studies sometimes name an engineer. Search the site for PDF files; those are where the names hide.

### 2. Job postings

A posting tells you who the new hire reports to ("reports to the Director of Operations"), which roles exist, and what is changing. A first-ever EHS manager posting, or a plant engineer posting that mentions a new line, is both a name lead and a buying signal. Postings also reveal the titles this company uses, which makes every other search easier.

### 3. Public records and filings

These depend on your market, but they are often the best source for companies that keep a low profile online:

- **Building and trade permits** list the applicant, the contractor and often the engineer or architect of record.
- **Environmental permits** (air, water, waste) usually name a facility contact or responsible official.
- **State business registrations** list officers and owners of private companies.
- **Contractor and professional licence registers** name the licence holder or qualifier at each firm.
- **Public procurement portals** name the contracting officer on each tender and, after award, the winning vendor.

### 4. Associations, conferences and trade press

Trade association directories, committee rosters and board lists carry names and titles. Conference agendas list speakers and panelists, and people who speak at a regional conference on wastewater compliance or warehouse automation are usually the ones who own that topic at their company. Trade press quotes do the same: a plant manager quoted about a new line is the person to know.

### 5. LinkedIn, used narrowly

LinkedIn is useful once you know which roles you want. Search the company page's people tab with the title variants from your role map, not just one title. Expect gaps: many plant, field and lab staff have sparse profiles. Treat what you find as a lead to confirm, not a fact.

### 6. The front desk

For industrial and trades companies, calling the main line and asking "Who looks after compressed air on site?" or "Who handles your fire alarm inspections?" still works more often than people expect. Ask for the name and the right way to reach them, not to be put through. Reception staff know who does what.

### 7. People who already know them

Your current customers, your distributors, the engineers who specify your product, and suppliers who sell to the same accounts all know names. Ask a narrow question: "Who runs maintenance at the Ridgeville plant now?" is easy to answer. "Can you introduce me to someone?" is not.

## Step 2: Verify before you reach out

A wrong name costs more in a small market than in a large one, because you only get a few chances at each account. Before a name goes on the list, check four things:

- **Still there.** A recent post, a recent quote, a recent permit or a current website listing. If the only evidence is two years old, call the front desk.
- **Title is current.** People move from plant engineer to plant manager. The new title changes the message.
- **Contact route works.** Check the email address with a verification tool, or use the company's published direct line. Do not guess at patterns and send.
- **Role in this purchase.** Write down which role from your map this person fills and how you know.

Follow the email and privacy rules of the countries you sell into, keep messages relevant to the person's job, and give every recipient an easy way to opt out.

## Worked example: a stack testing firm

Take a hypothetical firm that performs emissions testing for manufacturers. Its target account is a mid-sized coatings plant whose air permit is due for renewal and whose permit requires periodic stack tests.

- **The permit file** names the plant's environmental contact as the facility contact. That is the person with the problem.
- **The company website** has a press release about a new production line, quoting the plant manager. Economic owner.
- **A state manufacturers' association** environmental conference agenda lists the company's corporate environmental director as a speaker. Corporate layer, and possibly the person who approves testing vendors for every site.
- **A call to the front desk** confirms that purchasing for the site runs through a regional buyer and gives the buyer's name.
- **The permit application** was prepared by an outside environmental consultant, whose engineer often recommends testing firms. Influencer.

Five names, each with a source and a role, and none of them came from a contact database. The first message goes to the environmental contact and refers to the renewal timeline. The corporate director gets a separate note referring to the conference topic.

## Checklist

- We have written the five roles (problem, technical, economic, purchasing, blocker) for our sale, with the titles our market uses.
- We know how roles collapse at companies under 50 people and expand at multi-site companies.
- For each target account we have checked the website and PDFs, job postings, the public records that fit our market, association and conference lists, and LinkedIn.
- We call the front desk when the online trail runs out.
- Every name has a source, a date and a role in the purchase.
- Every contact route has been verified before the first message.
- We review the names on active accounts at least once a quarter, because people move.
