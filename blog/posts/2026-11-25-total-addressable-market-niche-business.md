Most advice on the total addressable market for a niche business was written for investor decks. It starts with an industry report ("the global industrial filtration market is worth $X billion"), takes a slice, takes a slice of the slice, and lands on a number that looks large and means very little. If you sell vibration analysis to paper mills or fire-protection engineering to cold-storage warehouses, that number will not tell you who to call on Monday.

For a niche B2B company, market size is not a dollar figure you put on a slide. It is a list. You can usually count every company that could buy from you, and the count is more useful than any percentage. This post shows how to do that count, how to turn it into a dollar figure if you need one, and how to use it to decide where your sales effort goes.

## Why top-down TAM breaks in a niche market

The standard method works top-down: total industry spend, times the share that fits your product, times the share you can reach. It has three problems when your market is small.

- **The categories are wrong.** Industry reports group spend by broad product class. "Industrial services" might include your calibration work alongside janitorial contracts. Your slice of that number is a guess on top of a guess.
- **It hides the count.** A $40 million market could be 4,000 buyers spending $10k or 40 buyers spending $1 million. Those are two completely different sales problems, and the dollar figure does not tell you which one you have.
- **It does not point anywhere.** Even if the number is right, it gives you no names. You still have to build the list.

A bottom-up count fixes all three. Data vendors describe the same basic formula: number of companies that fit your ideal customer profile, times what each would typically spend with you per year. The difference in a niche market is that you can actually do the first half by hand and check it.

## Step 1: Write the fit rules before you count anything

Start with a short definition of a company that could buy from you, written as rules someone else could apply without asking you questions. Four or five rules is usually enough.

- **What the company does.** Use the industry code (NAICS in the US and Canada) as a starting filter, then describe the activity in plain words, because codes are often wrong or too broad.
- **The physical or regulatory fact that creates the need.** A boiler over a certain size, a cleanroom, a permit type, an FDA registration, a licensed lab, a fleet over 50 vehicles. This is usually the best filter you have.
- **Size range.** Sites, employees or revenue, whichever best predicts whether they can afford you. Deals of $10k and up rule out the smallest operators in most industries.
- **Geography.** Where you can actually serve or sell.
- **Exclusions.** Companies you cannot sell to: tied to a competitor by ownership, captive buyers of a parent company, or too regulated in a way you do not support.

Example for an engineering firm that designs dust collection systems: "Facilities in the US that handle combustible dust (grain, wood, food powders, metals, plastics), with at least one site over 50 employees, not owned by a company that runs its own in-house engineering group." ## Step 2: Count from more than one source

No single source will give you the whole market. Use two or three and reconcile them.

1. **Government business counts.** The US Census Bureau's County Business Patterns publishes establishment counts by NAICS code, state and size class. It gives you no names, but it gives you a ceiling to check your list against.
2. **Regulatory and licensing databases.** Many niche markets are defined by a registration: FDA establishment registrations, EPA facility records, state contractor licences, accreditation body directories for labs, OSHA inspection records. These carry names and addresses, and the regulated fact is often the exact need you serve.
3. **Industry and association directories.** Trade associations, certification bodies and trade-show exhibitor lists cover companies that self-identify with the industry.
4. **Your own records.** Past customers, lost quotes, inbound inquiries and the names your salespeople already know. These test whether your rules are right: if a good past customer would fail your rules, fix the rules.

Merge the sources, remove duplicates (the same company appears under different names and at different addresses), and apply the exclusions. What remains is your addressable account list.

Expect this count to be smaller than you guessed. Owners of niche businesses often describe their market as "thousands of companies" and find a few hundred that actually fit once the rules are applied. That is not bad news. A market of a few hundred accounts can be covered properly by a small team.

## Step 3: Count sites and buyers, not just companies

In many niche markets the company is not the unit that buys. A food manufacturer with nine plants may buy sanitation chemicals at headquarters and maintenance services plant by plant. Decide which unit makes your purchase decision and count that.

- **Company-level purchases:** software, insurance, audits, corporate engineering standards.
- **Site-level purchases:** maintenance, repairs, local services, equipment replacement, testing.
- **Project-level purchases:** construction, expansions, retrofits. Here the count that matters is projects per year, not companies.

Then note how many people are involved in each purchase. In a niche B2B sale it is typically several: an operator or engineer with the problem, a manager who owns the budget, someone in procurement or finance, sometimes a safety or quality lead. ## Step 4: Put a dollar figure on it (only if you need one)

If a lender, partner or your own planning needs a dollar figure, build it from the list rather than from a report.

**Worked example (illustrative numbers):** a firm that does combustible dust hazard analysis and system design.

- Accounts that pass the fit rules after merging sources: 640 facilities.
- Typical first engagement (hazard analysis): about $15,000.
- How often a facility needs it: the relevant NFPA standards call for the hazard analysis to be reviewed and updated on a recurring cycle, roughly every five years, plus after significant process changes. Call it one engagement per facility every five years.
- Follow-on design work: about one in three analyses leads to a design project averaging $60,000.

Annual addressable revenue is then 640 ÷ 5 = 128 analyses a year × $15,000 = $1.92 million, plus about 43 design projects × $60,000 = $2.56 million. Total: roughly $4.5 million a year.

That number is small next to a top-down industry figure, and much more useful. It tells you that 128 facilities a year are due for the work, which is about two or three a week. Your sales problem is not reach. It is knowing which two or three, before a competitor does.

## Step 5: Split the market by timing

The count gives you the size of the market. Timing tells you which part of it is buying now. In most niche markets only a small share of accounts is in a buying window at any moment, and those windows are usually visible from the outside.

Sort your list into three groups and revisit it every week:

- **In market now.** A public signal shows a likely purchase: an expansion permit, a job posting for the role that owns your problem, a new plant manager, an inspection finding, a published RFP, a funding round, a lost certification. See our list of [B2B buying signals](/blog/b2b-buying-signals/) for what to track and where.
- **Due soon.** The account is near the end of a cycle you can estimate: a contract renewal, a recertification date, equipment reaching the end of its typical service life, a five-year review.
- **Not now.** Fits the rules, nothing indicates a purchase. Keep them on a low-effort contact rhythm and keep watching.

The in-market group is where direct outreach pays off. A focused message to the right four or five people at a facility that just filed for an expansion will usually do better than the same message sent to everyone on the list.

## Checklist: sizing your niche market in a week

- Write four or five fit rules, including the physical or regulatory fact that creates the need.
- Pull a ceiling count from government data for your industry codes and geography.
- Pull names from at least one regulatory or licensing source and one directory.
- Add your past customers, lost quotes and known accounts, and test the rules against them.
- Merge, deduplicate and apply exclusions. Record the final account count.
- Decide whether the company, site or project is the buying unit, and count that.
- Estimate typical first-deal size and how often an account buys. Multiply for an annual figure.
- Sort accounts into in market now, due soon and not now.
- Pick the signals that move an account into "in market now" and decide who checks them each week.
